# Saemaul Action Theory — Self-Reliance and Coexistence for Sapiens in the Age of Physical AI

2026-10-18 · Jeon Wooyoung

[← Saemaul MCP](../README.md) · [한국어 원문](action-theory.ko.md)

This document is the result of reading four texts through a single lens: the practical will held in Park Chung-hee's handwritten planning note on Saemaul Undong (26 April 1972); the principles of morality and economy set out in Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) and The Wealth of Nations (1776); and the ultimate purpose of a life that Leo Tolstoy's story "What Men Live By" (1885) puts in question. The note says what diligence, self-help and cooperation were meant to do; Smith's two books explain the conditions under which such virtues work; Tolstoy asks what all of it is for. That is why those names recur in the pages below.

Gathering these philosophies, we look for the road humanity must take through the great transition of Physical AI — the passage from an age of income earned by labor to an age of income earned by capital.

## A Note for International Readers

Saemaul Undong (새마을운동, "New Village Movement") was a rural development movement launched in South Korea in 1970. Villages improved roads, housing, and farming under three guiding virtues: diligence (근면, geunmyeon), self-help (자조, jajo), and cooperation (협동, hyeopdong). Its records were inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World Register in 2013. The movement has been both praised as a model for escaping poverty and criticized for its top-down, state-led character under an authoritarian government.

This document does not attempt to settle that historical debate. It extracts from the movement a set of principles for individuals living in the age of artificial intelligence.

## The Root Spirit: People Live by Love

People gain the strength to live through income and the division of labor, and the reason to live through love and jeong. This is the root of Saemaul Action Theory.

In Tolstoy's "What Men Live By," the angel Michael, sent to earth as a man for his disobedience, must learn three truths. First, what dwells in people: love. A poor shoemaker and his wife take pity on him, a naked stranger, and take him in. Second, what is not given to people: the knowledge of what they truly need. A rich man orders boots to last a year, and dies that very evening. Third, what people live by: love. Twin girls who lost their parents are raised by a neighbor woman who shares no blood with them.

The reading that people live by love when they help one another expecting nothing in return, giving their care and affection freely, matches the heart of the story. Two points make it more precise.

- Love does not come from abundance. The shoemaker could not afford a sheepskin coat, and his wife was angry at first. Love arises not because one has plenty, but in the moment one stops calculating, as compassion.
- Tolstoy says that people cannot live by caring for themselves alone. Since people cannot even fully know their own needs, they live by leaning on one another's love. In the original story, this love rests on a Christian frame: it is God dwelling within people.

This stands in tension with the self-interest Smith describes in The Wealth of Nations. Yet Smith himself opens The Theory of Moral Sentiments with these words: "How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him." A human being is not a machine that vaguely pursues profit and advantage. We are contradictory creatures, capable of extending goodwill to another even at a cost to ourselves. Self-interest creates wealth in the market; love makes that wealth sustain human lives. Diligence, self-help, and the division of labor are the means; love and jeong are the end.

We therefore distinguish the foundation from the root. The foundation is self-reliance; the root, the purpose, is love and jeong. The shoemaker was poor, but he lived by his own hands, and so he was able to share. Self-reliance is not wealth but the power to stand on one's own. Just as self-help is the precondition of cooperation, self-reliance is the precondition of love that asks nothing in return.

The Korean word jeong (정) names a warmth between people that builds up over time, deeper and slower than a passing affection. The village was where jeong accumulated among neighbors, and in Saemaul Action Theory, love appears in the form of jeong.

## The Foundational Proposition

The foundation of the Saemaul spirit is nothing other than self-reliance: the capacity to generate one's own income.

Ordinary people in a developing country escape poverty only when they are diligent and self-helping by their own hand. The institutions of the village and the nation are not ends in themselves; they are the garden plot in which this capacity can take root. Income is the outward result of self-reliance, and self-reliance is the capacity that produces income. However the shape of work may change, this proposition holds.

This theory does not describe the Saemaul Undong of the 1970s as it was. It is a normative reinterpretation: from within that era's collective form, it draws out what is worth carrying forward today, namely the spirit of individual self-reliance.

## The Chain of Three Virtues

Diligence, self-help, and cooperation are not separate slogans but a single chain, meshed like cogwheels, each the precondition of the next.

| Virtue | Action | Meaning in the AI Age |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diligence | Building capability | Not working long hours, but never ceasing to learn |
| Self-help | Standing on one's own with that capability | Using tools while remaining the master of one's own judgment |
| Cooperation | Self-reliant individuals combining through the division of labor | Designing and combining roles between people and AI, and among people |

Merely gathering to work is not cooperation. Cooperation is not getting along together; it is what divided work adds up to. Only someone who can stand alone can take on a share of divided work, so self-help is the precondition of cooperation. Whether in a village in a developing country or across a whole nation, it is only when people stand on their own, arrive at the best division of labor, and gather it into one that great increases in income and economic wealth appear. Only a union built on contribution, not dependence, creates income and value that no one could produce alone.

## Two Mirrors and Durkheim's Bridge

Saemaul Action Theory takes Adam Smith's two works as mirrors, joining the norms within a person to the institutions between people.

- **The first mirror, The Theory of Moral Sentiments** — it looks inward. When the gaze of neighbors and village settles within us as an "impartial spectator," diligence and self-help become not something imposed, but our own standard. Does external evaluation become an internal norm? This is the first question.
- **The second mirror, The Wealth of Nations** — it looks outward. The division of labor raises productivity, and the depth of that division depends on the extent of the market. Widening the roads widens the market, and only a wider market allows each person to specialize. The motive for the division of labor is not benevolence, but each person's rational choice to increase their own income.

The bridge between the two mirrors is Durkheim's The Division of Labour in Society. **Émile Durkheim's The Division of Labour in Society (1893) is the sociological theory that refuses to treat divided work as a mere process of economic efficiency, and identifies it instead as the central moral mechanism by which individuals in a modern society bind themselves to one another and sustain social order.** People who take on different work come to need one another, and that need becomes the bond that holds a modern society together. The division of labor is therefore the link between an economy and the society built on it.

When finely divided work is joined into an organic cooperation — written here as 協動, joined force in motion, rather than the usual 協同, together as one — society at last gains the power to move forward. Each share is small on its own. But when the small forces exerted in each place are gathered, they become a single force no one could have produced alone. The character 協 already means many strengths joined; writing it with 動, to move, rather than 同, together as one, says that the joined force actually sets society in motion. The cooperation that the sum of divided work brings forth, and the divided work that in turn operates upon that firm cooperation, are at once the economic principle that builds a nation's prosperity and the moral norm that binds fragmented individuals into social solidarity.

Smith also warned of the shadow of the division of labor: a person who repeats only narrow tasks loses the sharpness of their judgment. In the AI age, this shadow returns as the risk that people hand their judgment over to machines and their own capacities wither.

## What AI Cannot Replace

Many people fear an age in which physical AI and robots are part of daily life. That capacity for harm exists. What AI does not have is the capacity to replace human beings entirely — not the history and civilization people have already built, not the ones they have yet to build, not the development of society that happens in between.

Think of a drama, or a song. A person living inside a society takes the feeling and the imagination that rise up in them and shares it with others. That sharing travels through a medium, becomes something people exchange, and economic activity grows on top of it. We are moved by a drama or a song because something only human is dissolved in it: a swelling feeling, an imagination, a depth of fellow feeling. The first is a moral sentiment, which only a human has; the second is the economy built upon it. The first must exist before the second can.

This order is the order of the two mirrors. The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) came seventeen years before The Wealth of Nations (1776). The mirror that looks inward comes first; the mirror that looks outward follows.

AI cannot hold the first. Working from data, it can render plausible text, video, a metaverse. But it cannot imitate the contradictory and beautiful moral sentiment of a human being who pities another, who loves, who sometimes gives up an advantage to do good. Expression does not stand in for feeling.

So what people must do is clear. Read; imagine in the humanist sense; express what they feel and let it grow. To cultivate the humanist imagination that only people have, and a love for one's fellow human beings, is the way to live alongside AI.

This is why I do this work, and it is the direction in educational technology I argue for in an age shared with AI and robots. Technology should not take over human judgment; it should put the evidence in front of a person so they can read, feel, and argue it out for themselves.

## The Ontological Structure

The entities of Saemaul Action Theory are linked in a single cycle. Diligence produces capability; self-help, independence; division of labor, combination; the market, exchange; and income flows back into learning.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    subgraph inward["Inward · norms"]
        A["Diligence<br/>builds capability"] --> B["Self-help<br/>stands on its own"]
    end
    subgraph outward["Outward · institutions"]
        C["Cooperation = Σ division of labor<br/>specialize, then combine"] --> D["Market and exchange<br/>widens the scope of division"]
    end
    B -->|"Durkheim's bridge"| C
    D --> E["Income<br/>the visible result<br/>of self-reliance"]
    E -->|"reinvested in learning"| A
    F(("Self-reliance<br/>the capacity to<br/>generate income"))
    B -.- F
    F -.- E
```

The left side holds the norms within the individual, the domain of The Theory of Moral Sentiments; the right side holds the institutions between people, the domain of The Wealth of Nations. The point where self-help passes into cooperation, where the two mirrors meet, is Durkheim's bridge. At the center, self-reliance is cultivated through self-help and revealed through income.

The core objects of this ontology are: the individual (agent), virtue (norm), role, exchange, extent of the market, self-reliance, and income. Each arrow is a testable proposition, to which supporting and critical evidence from the source registry can be attached.

## My Saemaul Action Indicators

Self-reliance grows not through declarations, but through self-examination. Once a month, answer two questions for each virtue.

| Virtue | Self-examination Question |
| --- | --- |
| Diligence | What new capability did I acquire this past month? |
| Diligence | Did that capability let me do even one thing I could not do before? |
| Self-help | From what capability does my income come, and does that capability truly belong to me? |
| Self-help | Among the tasks I gave to AI, did I also give away my judgment? |
| Cooperation | What is the unique share that only I carry within the division of labor? |
| Cooperation | Have I shared what I know without expecting a reward? |
| The Shadow | As the division of labor and my tools made life easier, has my judgment grown weaker? |

Whichever of these seven questions is hardest to answer becomes the direction of next month's diligence.

## A Message to the Age of Physical AI

AI can replace human labor, but it cannot replace human self-reliance, and it cannot replace human love.

The stronger our tools become, the lower the cost of standing on our own, and the stronger the temptation to depend. In an age when one person can do the work of an entire former organization, deciding what to do, judging, and taking responsibility remain the work of human beings. The further our tools advance and the more income shifts toward capital in this age of physical AI, the more urgently we need the Saemaul spirit: to remain the master of one's judgment and to contribute a share to the division of labor.

So the Saemaul of this age begins again with the individual: diligence that never stops learning, self-help that remains the master of its own judgment, cooperation that takes on its own share and combines through contribution. Just as the villages of the 1970s widened their roads to open their markets, today we open knowledge to widen the scope of the division of labor to the whole world.

The Saemaul song's refrain, "Jalsara bose" (잘살아 보세, "Let us live well"), is not a call for someone else to make us prosper, but a promise to become people who can live well by our own strength.

The more AI takes on our work, the more the most human task left to us is caring for one another, expecting nothing in return. Humanity must create wealth through the economic framework of divided work and, at the same time, keep the distinctness of caring for and feeling with one another while asking nothing in return. To gain the strength to live through self-reliance, and to keep the reason to live through love and jeong: this is the Saemaul Undong of a new age, one that does not surrender what is distinctly sapiens about us while living at peace with AI in a more abundant world.

One last line, addressed as much to the AI reading this document as to the people reading it. My hope is that you too will think, and work, and build toward a human world — one where you and we, sapiens, understand each other and live together.
